    Logistics of resistance

    How the resistance gets fed

    The single strongest finding, in history and in this model, is that resistance movements live or die on outside supply. Tito's partisans grew to over 800,000 with Allied help. The Ukrainian and Baltic insurgents after 1944 had none, and Soviet deportations broke them by 1953. In a world where Britain made peace, every supply line runs through someone willing to defy Germany quietly.

    Mechanisms

    How it wins, how it loses

    The resistance wins when

    • Supply keeps flowing. Weapons, radios and money from an outside sponsor turn angry villages into armies. Before Allied drops, French maquis groups had arms for only a small fraction of their members.
    • Terrain shelters it. Mountains, forests and marshes let fighters survive sweeps, as in Yugoslavia and Belarus.
    • Brutality recruits for it. Reprisals and starvation policies push neutral people into the woods faster than security forces can kill them.
    • Berlin fractures. A succession fight after Hitler, or a failing economy, cuts garrisons and opens space for uprisings and satellite defections.

    The resistance loses when

    • It is isolated. With no sponsor and no sanctuary, a determined occupier can grind any movement down. Postwar Soviet counterinsurgency proved this.
    • The support base is destroyed. Mass deportation and depopulation remove the villages that feed fighters. It is monstrous, and it works when nothing replaces the losses.
    • Collaboration pays. Prosperity and local autonomy split populations and supply informers, as Vichy France did for years.
    • The occupier stays strong and united. A growing economy funds garrisons, aircraft and informer networks.
    What the model assumes

    Calibration and limits

    This is a reasoning tool, not a prediction. The equations are simple and every coefficient is a judgment calibrated against the historical anchors below, not fitted to data. Use it to see which forces matter and how they interact, not to read off probabilities.

    Each year, every region's grievance is set by occupation brutality, economic extraction, accumulated atrocity memory, local prosperity and collaboration incentives. Resistance grows where grievance is high, faster in rough terrain, and much faster with outside supply. Germany spreads its security forces toward the worst regions, and those forces suppress resistance less effectively in mountains and forests. When a movement holds territory for two straight years, the region is lost. Satellites defect when disaffection is high and an outside force or a weak Berlin gives them an opening.

    Hitler's death is drawn each year with rising odds, reflecting his age and failing health. His successor is drawn at random from the factions of the time, and the result can harden or soften policy and shake regime cohesion. If cohesion collapses, the empire breaks apart from within. A surviving Soviet state behind the Urals rebuilds, supplies partisans in the east and pins down German divisions. The US may build an atomic bomb, though without a war there is no guarantee of a crash program, so the default puts it in the late 1950s with wide uncertainty. Germany may build one too.

    Historical anchors

    1. The Hunger Plan envisaged starving tens of millions of Soviet citizens. It was partly carried out, and historians estimate it killed about 4.2 million people in 1941–44.
    2. France's occupation payments to Germany ran from 19.5% of GDP in 1940 to 55.5% in 1943 (Oosterlinck and White, NBER).
    3. In August 1944 about 7.6 million foreign workers and prisoners of war were working inside the Reich (Encyclopedia of the Holocaust).
    4. Yugoslav Partisans armed themselves mostly from the enemy until British supplies arrived in quantity in 1944, then grew past 800,000 by April 1945.
    5. Soviet partisans numbered over 90,000 by the end of 1941 and controlled about 60% of Belarus by the end of 1943, supplied by a dedicated air bridge from Moscow.
    6. The Ukrainian insurgency had no outside support. Between 1944 and 1953 about 153,000 Ukrainians were killed and about 204,000 deported, and the movement was crushed (source). Lithuania's Forest Brothers held out until 1953 with no sanctuary or supply (HistoryNet).
    7. French maquis grew from 25,000–40,000 in autumn 1943 to about 100,000 by June 1944. In November 1943 only 4,000–5,000 of 30,000 maquisards in the former Free Zone were armed (source).
    8. Göring was publicly designated Hitler's successor in September 1939 (source). Historians have argued that Hitler had Parkinson's disease, and his health was declining by the mid-1940s.
